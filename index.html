▾ Understanding CAN

One pair of wires, every module

A CAN (Controller Area Network) bus lets every module on the truck share data over two wires instead of running a separate wire for every signal. The ECM, TCM, ABS, cluster and aftertreatment module all connect in parallel to the same backbone through short stubs.

  • CAN-H (yellow on most trucks) and CAN-L (green) are twisted together. Noise picked up by the pair hits both wires equally, and the modules only look at the difference between them, so the noise cancels out.
  • A 120 Ω terminating resistor sits at each end of the backbone. The two in parallel measure 60 Ω. They soak up the signal at the ends so it doesn't bounce back and corrupt the next bit.
  • There is no master. Any module can talk when the bus is quiet, and every module hears every message. Each one keeps only the messages it needs.
  • If two modules start at once, the message with the lower ID wins (higher priority) and the other waits. Nothing is lost.
  • Every receiver checks each frame (CRC) and acknowledges it. A module that keeps making errors takes itself off the bus (bus-off) so it can't jam the network.

What the voltages do

  • Recessive (logic 1, bus idle): CAN-H and CAN-L both sit near 2.5 V. Difference about 0 V.
  • Dominant (logic 0): CAN-H rises to about 3.5 V and CAN-L drops to about 1.5 V. Difference about 2 V.
  • A meter is too slow to see the bits. It shows an average: CAN-H a little above 2.5 V, CAN-L a little below. A scope shows the real square waves, and CAN-H and CAN-L should be mirror images.
Because the modules read the difference, CAN can sometimes keep working with one wire damaged, but often with errors. Never assume a "working" bus is a healthy one. Measure it.

CAN is the delivery system: how the bits travel on the wires, how modules take turns and how errors are caught. It says nothing about what the data means. J1939 is the language heavy trucks speak on top of CAN. It defines who each module is, what each message contains and how fault codes are reported. Every J1939 network is a CAN network, but not every CAN network is J1939.

Short to ground

The wire touches chassis or engine ground (chafed through on a bracket, crushed in a clip). The voltage on the wire is pulled toward 0 V.

  • Sensor signal wire: reads near 0 V, below the 0.5 V minimum → FMI 4 (voltage below normal or shorted low).
  • 5 V reference: pulls the whole supply down. Every sensor on that supply sets a code at once, plus a supply code (SPN 3509 or 3510).
  • Power feed: blows the fuse or trips the breaker.
  • CAN-H to ground: CAN-H reads near 0 V and the bus usually goes down. No module can talk.
  • How to prove it: key off, unplug both ends of the wire, ohms from the wire to chassis ground. Good = OL. Shorted = near 0 Ω.

Short to power (battery voltage)

The wire touches a battery-voltage circuit, usually another wire in the same harness that has rubbed through. The voltage is pulled up toward 12–14 V.

  • Sensor signal wire: reads battery voltage, way above the 4.5 V maximum → FMI 3 (voltage above normal or shorted high). It can damage the ECM input.
  • Short to the 5 V reference (signal touching ref) reads about 5 V. Also FMI 3.
  • CAN-H or CAN-L to power: the wire sits at 12–14 V and the bus goes down. It can damage transceivers.
  • How to prove it: use volts, not ohms. Key on, unplug the sensor, measure the signal wire to ground. It should be near 0 V (or the module's pull-up voltage, never more than 5 V). Battery voltage there means a short to power.
  • Never use the ohmmeter on a powered circuit. Find which power wire it is shorted to with the battery disconnected, ohming between the two wires.

Step by step

  1. Find out who isn't talking. Connect the scan tool and look at the cluster. Which modules are missing? Look for SPN 639 (J1939 network) codes and "no data from…" messages.
  2. Key off, check the backbone: C to D at the 9-pin should be about 60 Ω. This one reading checks both terminators and most of the backbone.
  3. Check each wire to ground: C to A and D to A should not be near 0 Ω.
  4. Key on, check the voltages: CAN-H 2.5–3.0 V, CAN-L 2.0–2.5 V. Near 0 V or battery voltage points to a short.
  5. One module missing? Check its power and ground first (voltage-drop test). Then unplug it and measure H to L at its harness connector: 60 Ω proves its stub reaches the backbone.
  6. Whole bus down? Unplug modules one at a time. If communication comes back, that module or its stub is pulling the bus down.
  7. Intermittent? Wiggle-test the harness while watching the voltages, or use a scope. Look for H and L mirror images with clean square edges.

Good habits

  • Always measure resistance with the key off. A powered bus gives false ohm readings.
  • Back-probe connectors. Don't pierce CAN wires: it breaks the shield and twist and lets water in.
  • Keep repairs twisted, with about one twist per inch. Never run CAN wires untwisted or splice in regular wire.
  • Some modules have a terminating resistor built in. Check the wiring diagram before adding or removing one.
  • Clear network codes only after the repair, then confirm every module is back on the scan tool.
  • A module that drops off only when the engine runs often has a power or ground problem, not a CAN wiring problem.
Practice: go to Faults → Network Wiring, pull a terminator or pick a wiring fault, then work through these steps with the meter on the 9-pin connector.

    Common heavy-duty J1939 fault codes

    SPN meanings are standard, but the FMI an OEM sets for a given failure, its lamp, and its derate behavior vary by make and model year (for example, Detroit reports low rail pressure as 157/16 where another OEM uses 157/18). Look up the exact code in the OEM troubleshooting guide before testing. Codes marked "secondary" are effects: fix the root-cause code first.

    FMI reference · SAE J1939-73

    A J1939 fault code is SA + SPN + FMI (plus an occurrence count). SA is the module that set it, SPN is the part or parameter, and FMI is how it failed. FMIs 3, 4, 5 and 6 usually point to a circuit problem. FMIs 0, 1 and 15–18 usually mean the sensor is reading correctly and the real condition is out of range, so verify it mechanically (for example, with an oil pressure gauge).

    Accuracy notes

    • Message layouts follow SAE J1939-71/-73 (J1939DA): EEC1 61444, EEC2 61443, ET1 65262, EFL/P1 65263, IC1 65270, EGF1 61450, LFE1 65266, CCVS 65265, Dash Display 65276, ETC1 61442, ETC2 61445, EBC1 61441, EBC2 65215, DEF tank 65110, DM1 65226, DM2 65227, DM3 65228, DM11 65235, Request 59904, Acknowledgment 59392, TP.CM 60416, TP.DT 60160. Bytes not used here are sent as 0xFF ("not available"); a real module fills many of them.
    • The vehicle model is simplified: a 10-speed transmission, 3.36 axle ratio, about 500 tire revolutions per mile, and approximate boost, airflow, fuel rate and rail pressure for each load. Numbers are realistic in shape, not specific to any engine.
    • SPN numbers are standard, but which SPN an OEM uses for a part, and which FMI it sets for a given failure, varies. Here, crank = SPN 190 and cam = SPN 723 (Cummins style). Detroit uses 636 for crank and 723 for cam; Mack/Volvo use 637 for crank and 636 for cam. Wheel sensor FMIs follow Bendix (FMI 1 air gap, 7 wheel end, 8 erratic) and WABCO (FMI 5 open, 6 shorted). Which sensors share a 5 V supply (SPN 3509/3510) is engine-specific. Always use the OEM fault tree.
    • On some engines the ECM reports aftertreatment faults itself instead of a separate ACM, and NOx sensors often sit on a private aftertreatment CAN.
    • Lamp choices are typical for HD-OBD (2010+) trucks. Detection times are shortened so the demo moves quickly. Multimeter readings come from a simplified circuit model; use OEM specs for real values.
